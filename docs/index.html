Stormy

Wet, windy Thanksgiving with coastal flooding threat through Friday; unsettled into early next week.

7 watches, warnings, and advisories

  • Coastal flood watch for Seattle, the Puget Sound shoreline, and the north coast from late tonight through Thursday afternoon
  • Coastal flood warning for parts of the Strait of Juan de Fuca and the outer coast until 4 PM today

A series of Pacific weather systems will keep the pattern active through the end of the week. A warm front lifts north today, followed by a cold front Thursday. A weaker system arrives Friday, riding over a ridge of high pressure building toward California. Forecasters expect a short lull over the weekend, then another run of systems returns early next week. High tides, storm surge, and large surf are adding to the coastal threat.

For most of western Washington, that means a wet, windy Thanksgiving. Gusts near 50 mph are possible on the coast and in the northern interior, with breezy conditions in Puget Sound. Rain totals run from about an inch in the lowlands to several inches in the Olympics and Cascades. Winds ease Thursday afternoon, but coastal flooding remains the bigger concern through Friday, and a river in Mason County is expected to reach flood stage midweek.

How confident forecasters are

Forecasters say the overall picture has changed little, so the Thursday storm and the coastal flooding are well supported. The weekend break is less certain, since ensemble forecasts (groups of model runs) show a lull, but forecasters doubt it would stay dry for long.

Terms in this discussion
Warm front
The leading edge of warmer air pushing into colder air, usually bringing steady rain and a shift in wind.
Cold front
The leading edge of colder air pushing under warmer air, often bringing a brief burst of showers and gusty winds, then clearing.
Upper ridge
An elongated area of high pressure high in the atmosphere, which tends to push storms away and bring drier weather at the surface.
Storm surge
Water pushed toward shore by strong winds and low air pressure, which adds to the height of the tide.
Ensemble forecasts
A set of computer model runs with slightly different starting conditions, used to show the range of possible outcomes.
Rain shadow
The drier area on the far side of mountains from the prevailing wind, which receives much less rain than nearby higher ground.

Forecasters began the week expecting a few quiet, foggy days under a strong ridge of high pressure aloft (a dome of high pressure high in the atmosphere). They were also confident that it would fade over the weekend, with light rain and mountain snow showers as a cold low (an upper low) drifted offshore and stalled. The bigger story was always what came next: a broad dip in the jet stream over the northeast Pacific (a trough) that would send a string of storms toward Washington. That call held steady all week. What changed was the details. Forecasters grew more confident about strong coastal winds and high seas, and the coastal flooding worry moved around, first midweek, then Monday morning's high tide, then Tuesday. By midweek the peak had shifted to late Wednesday into Thursday, when some of the year's highest tides were expected to line up with the storms.

By Tuesday and Wednesday the forecast pointed to a wet, windy Thanksgiving, with about an inch of rain in the lowlands and several inches in the Olympics and Cascades. Coastal flooding was expected to remain the biggest concern through Friday, and the Skokomish River was expected to reach flood stage. Snow levels stayed high, so rain fell at most elevations, but the North Cascades could still get heavy snow. Forecasters were never confident about a possible weekend lull under building high pressure. Its timing slipped from Friday to the weekend, and they still saw more storms likely early next week.

The past month

Cool, quiet late October gave way to a stormy, flood-prone November pattern

Covers Sun Oct 25 to Mon Nov 23.

The period began with a cool, unsettled pattern. A cold pocket of air aloft (an upper-level low) lingered near the coast, and a front brought the first widespread Cascade snow around October 27-28. Forecasters were confident about that system, and a Winter Weather Advisory covered the Cascades. After it, high pressure was expected to settle in. Forecasters raised the frost and freeze threat, then added dense fog for Halloween weekend, and the first days of November stayed mostly quiet.

The outlook shifted around November 2-3. Forecasters began pointing to a string of Pacific storms, with a first strong system on Friday, November 6, and a stronger one Saturday night into Sunday. Their confidence grew through the week that the second would be a real windstorm, with strong coastal gusts, possible scattered power outages (trees still had leaves), and high surf. From there the pattern stayed wet and mild, fed at times by a long plume of moisture called an atmospheric river. Repeated rain brought flood worries for the Skokomish River and high-tide flooding along the coast. Forecasters grew less sure about each later storm's strength and timing.

A drier spell looked likely in mid-November, with a stalled area of high pressure (a blocking pattern) and a deep low that was expected to slide south of Washington. That brought a few foggy, dry days. Forecasters held that view, though they briefly wavered on the midweek low. By November 20-23 they were confident a wet, windy stretch was returning. The current outlook is a series of storms through about Thursday, with coastal flooding and high surf the main concern around Wednesday's high tides. Heavy rain and snow are expected on the Olympics and in the North Cascades. A break comes Friday, and winds are expected to return next weekend. Forecasters are less sure how strong the Wednesday-into-Thursday system will be.
