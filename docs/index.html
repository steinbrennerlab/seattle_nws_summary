A strong cold front brings heavy rain, damaging wind gusts, and dangerous coastal surf tonight into Sunday, with more storms next week.

  • High Wind Warning for the Grays Harbor County coast and Northern Washington coast, from 4 PM Saturday to 4 AM Sunday.
  • Wind Advisory for interior lowlands, including Seattle, Everett/Marysville, the Eastside, Island County, Pierce and southern King County lowlands, western Skagit, northwestern Snohomish, and western Whatcom lowlands, Port Townsend, and San Juan County, from 6 PM Saturday to 4 AM Sunday.

  • High Surf Warning for the Grays Harbor County coast and Northern Washington coast, from 10 PM Saturday to 10 PM Sunday.
  • Gale Warnings for Puget Sound, Hood Canal, Admiralty Inlet, the San Juan Islands area, and the Strait of Juan de Fuca, ending between 4 AM and 10 AM Sunday.
  • Storm Warning for the coastal waters, until 10 AM Sunday.
  • Small Craft Advisory for the Grays Harbor Bar until 4 PM Sunday.

A strong cold front crosses western Washington from this evening into Sunday morning. Expect widespread lowland rain, gusty south winds, mountain snow at higher elevations, and very high surf on the coast. Forecasters give a 20-30% chance of isolated thunderstorms, mainly along the coast, and a lower 10-20% chance in the interior, where a lightning strike or two is possible.

Winds are the main concern overnight. Forecasters say there is a 50-70% chance of gusts over 55 mph along the coast, so a High Wind Warning is in effect there. Interior areas are under a Wind Advisory for gusts of 45 to 50 mph at times. Many trees still have leaves, so localized power outages are possible. Heavy rain overlapping with the wind could clog storm drains this evening, so give yourself extra travel time. Winds peak overnight into early Sunday morning and ease steadily by late Sunday afternoon. Waves of 22 to 28 feet are expected along the beaches tonight, and a High Surf Warning is in effect. Razor clam diggers should be cautious, since big waves and strong wind make the dig dangerous.

On Sunday the steady rain turns to showers, mostly over the North Cascades and Olympics, and the snow level drops from around 7000-7500 feet to 4500 feet, bringing some snow to higher mountain terrain. Highs are in the mid 50s. After a break Sunday evening, another system arrives Monday with widespread rain and locally breezy conditions, again with the most rain over the Olympics. Wet, breezy weather continues Tuesday into Wednesday, with warmer temperatures, and more systems are expected late in the week. River flooding is not expected in the next seven days, but repeated rain will saturate soils and raise river flows, which could become a concern with storms late next week.

How confident forecasters are

Forecasters are fairly confident in the wind and rain tonight, enough to keep the High Wind Warning and Wind Advisories in place. Thunderstorms are a lower-odds possibility, at 20-30% on the coast and 10-20% inland. Forecasters describe computer model guidance for next week as "a bit shaky" on the timing and strength of the systems, so those details are less certain, though an active pattern looks likely.

Terms in this discussion
Cold front
The leading edge of a colder air mass. Its passage often brings a burst of rain, a shift in wind direction, and gusty wind.
Instability
A condition in which air rises easily and can build towering clouds. It is what allows thunderstorms to form.
REFS
The Rapid Refresh Forecast System, a set of computer model runs used to estimate how likely different outcomes, such as strong wind gusts, are.
High Wind Warning
An alert for sustained or gusting wind strong enough to cause damage, such as downed trees and power outages.
Wind Advisory
An alert for wind strong enough to be a nuisance and possibly cause minor damage, a step below a High Wind Warning.
High Surf Warning
An alert for large, dangerous waves along the coast that can sweep people off beaches and jetties.
Snow level
The elevation above which precipitation falls as snow rather than rain.
QPF
Quantitative precipitation forecast, meaning the predicted amount of rain or melted snow.
Ensemble guidance
A group of computer forecasts run with slightly different starting conditions. When they disagree, confidence in the details is lower.
Base flows
The normal, steady level of water in rivers. Wet soils push these higher, so later rain can lead to flooding more easily.

The past week

Forecasters saw a stormy week coming early, then narrowed it to Friday's wind and a stronger Saturday night windstorm.

Covers Sun Nov 1 to Sat Nov 7.

On Sunday, Nov 1, the forecast was fog and clouds Monday, then wetter, windier weather from Wednesday. Forecasters also flagged a possible heavy-rain event for the next weekend, though they said it was uncertain. By Monday night they were calling a Friday storm possibly the strongest, with about a 50% chance of damaging gusts. On Tuesday, the stronger storm's start moved from Thursday night to Friday, and they added the risk of power outages because many trees still have leaves. The odds of gusts over 40 mph on the northern coast and from Whidbey Island northward rose to as high as 80%.

The biggest change came midweek. On Wednesday, forecasters added a second, separate storm for Saturday night into Sunday. By Thursday, Friday's wind was fairly well pinned down, and Wind Advisories were issued for the coast, islands and northern interior. Saturday night's wind went from a 50% chance of 50 mph gusts on the coast and in the north to likely advisory-level winds, possibly reaching Puget Sound. On Friday, forecasters put a 50 to 75% chance on gusts above 58 mph, the High Wind Warning threshold, and issued a High Wind Watch and a High Surf Advisory. By Saturday, the watch had become a High Wind Warning for the coast. Seattle and the interior lowlands were under a Wind Advisory for gusts of 45 to 50 mph at times. Forecasters also added a 10-20% chance of thunderstorms in the interior and a warning about clogged storm drains from heavy rain during the wind.

Some things barely changed all week. Snow levels stayed high, so snow was limited to the upper mountains. River flooding was not expected in the following seven days, though repeated rain will soak the ground and raise rivers. An active, wet pattern for the week of Nov 9 appeared in nearly every discussion. The details never firmed up. On Saturday, forecasters called the computer-model guidance for next week "a bit shaky" on timing and strength.

The past month

Forecasts shifted from frosty, dry autumn calm to a strong windstorm expected Saturday night, November 7.

Covers Fri Oct 9 to Sat Nov 7.

The period opened with the season's first frost. On Oct 9 forecasters issued the first Frost Advisory for southwest Washington foothills and valleys, and frost advisories followed for the next two mornings. Midweek rain was the big question. On Oct 10 forecasters said many computer model runs kept western Washington mostly dry. By Oct 12 and 13 they expected only light showers, mostly north and on the coast. A warm, dry spell then took shape for Oct 15 and 16, with highs in the mid to upper 70s. Forecasts for the weekend system after it were trimmed repeatedly, from a wetter Saturday to just a few hundredths of an inch in the lowlands.

The second half of October was mostly quiet, and forecasters kept lowering the odds of anything significant. A system near California stayed offshore. On Oct 20 forecasters said an early-weekend system was weak and its track a toss-up. They put early the next week at 49% for high pressure and 51% for a cooler, unsettled trough. By Oct 22 and 23 they were also noting early, low-confidence hints (a 20 to 40% chance of heavier rain) of a more active pattern around Halloween into early November.

The first real change came Oct 24. A forecast for light rain became the season's first fall-like storm. Forecasters expected 1 to 2 inches of rain on the coast and Olympic Peninsula and breezy winds, followed by another system Tuesday night into Wednesday. Mountain snow forecasts firmed up. On Oct 25 they gave site-by-site odds of heavy snow, and on Oct 26 they issued a Winter Weather Advisory for the Cascades. Totals were 6 to 12 inches in the southern Cascades but only up to 3 inches at Snoqualmie Pass. A small lowland thunderstorm chance came and went. After that, cold took over. A Freeze Watch (Oct 29) became a Freeze Warning for the Lewis and southern Thurston County lowlands and Middle Chehalis Valley (Oct 30), with a Frost Advisory farther north. Dense fog followed on Oct 31. Forecasters then backed off frost and fog talk on Nov 1, when clouds raised the expected lows above freezing.

The focus in early November was wind. Forecasters first flagged a stronger storm for Thursday night into Friday. On Nov 3 they moved it to Friday and added a risk of power outages, since many trees still have leaves. By Nov 5 they were also warning about a stronger front Saturday night. Wind Advisories were issued for Friday in the northern coast, islands and northern interior, and forecasters called Friday's wind forecast fairly high confidence. On Nov 6 they put a 50 to 75% chance on gusts above 58 mph, the High Wind Warning threshold, and issued a High Wind Watch for the coast. On Nov 7 the watch became a High Wind Warning for the coast and a Wind Advisory including Seattle, along with a High Surf Warning. More wet systems are expected next week, but forecasters call the timing and strength "a bit shaky." River flooding is not expected in the next seven days, though repeated rain will soak the ground.
